PCOS and hair loss: why it happens and what actually helps

You noticed it in the shower first. Then on your pillow. Then in the hairbrush, more than usual. And somewhere in between, you started to worry.

You have probably already tried a new shampoo. Maybe a hair oil your aunt swore by, or a biotin supplement from the pharmacy. If none of it made a real difference, that is not because you did something wrong. It is because hair fall from PCOS does not start on your scalp. It starts with your hormones.

It is not just stress, and it is not just genetics

Stress can thin hair. So can family history. You have probably heard both explanations, and neither one fully explained what you were seeing. That is a fair thing to notice. With PCOS, there is usually a third factor running underneath the other two, and it rarely gets mentioned in a five minute doctor visit.

What PCOS is actually doing to your hair

PCOS can raise the level of androgens in your body, hormones like testosterone that every woman has, just usually in smaller amounts. When androgens run higher than your body is used to, they can shrink the hair follicles on your scalp over time. The hair that grows back from a shrunken follicle is finer and weaker, until eventually some follicles stop producing hair you can see at all.

This usually shows up as thinning at the crown or a widening part, rather than bald patches. It can be gradual enough that you only notice it properly in photos, or when you gather your hair and it feels like less than it used to.

Insulin plays a part too

High insulin, which is common with PCOS, can push your ovaries to produce more androgens. So the same insulin resistance that makes fat loss harder can also be quietly feeding the hair fall. This is why fixing hair loss with a topical treatment alone often falls short. The signal causing it is coming from inside the body, not from the scalp itself.

It depends on your hormonal type

Not every woman with PCOS loses hair, and among those who do, it does not look the same for everyone. Some notice it mainly around ovulation or before their period. Others see it more steadily through the month. How much androgens are involved for you, versus insulin or thyroid function, changes what actually moves the needle. This is exactly why generic hair vitamins work for some women and do nothing for others.

So what actually helps?

Hair regrowth is slow no matter what you do, because hair grows in cycles that take months, not days. But you can support the right things while you wait for that to show.

  • Work on insulin, not just hair. Steadier blood sugar through the day can lower the androgen signal that is shrinking your follicles.
  • Do not crash diet. A steep calorie deficit is a stress signal to your body, and stress hormones can make hair fall worse, not better.
  • Be patient with any change you make. A new habit needs a few hair cycles, usually a few months, before you can judge whether it worked.
  • Get your levels checked. Thyroid, iron and vitamin D all affect hair, and it helps to know which of these are actually off for you before guessing.

None of this means you have to live with it. It means the plan has to start with your hormones, not with a bottle from the pharmacy.
