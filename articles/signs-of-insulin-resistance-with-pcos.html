Signs of insulin resistance with PCOS

You crash by 4pm most days. You wake up hungry an hour after breakfast. You crave something sweet the moment your energy dips. None of this is you being weak. It is very likely your insulin talking.

Most women with PCOS have heard the word "insulin resistance" thrown around at some point, usually without anyone explaining what it actually feels like day to day. It is not just a lab number. It shows up in your energy, your cravings and your weight, long before a blood test ever confirms it.

What insulin resistance actually is

Insulin is the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your cells for energy. With PCOS, your cells often stop listening to insulin as well as they should. So your body compensates by pumping out more of it. That extra insulin does two things at once. It pushes your body to store fat, especially around your middle. And it makes it much harder to burn that fat back off, no matter how carefully you eat.

This is why the standard advice, eat less and move more, so often falls flat for women with PCOS. It treats every body as if it runs on the same rules. Yours may not.

The signs that tend to show up first

Insulin resistance rarely announces itself clearly. It shows up as a pattern of small, easy to dismiss things.

Energy crashes after meals

If you feel sleepy or foggy about an hour after eating, especially after carb heavy meals, that is often your blood sugar spiking and then dropping hard. It is not a lack of sleep. It is your body overcorrecting.

Sugar cravings that feel impossible to ignore

When blood sugar crashes, your brain reads it as an emergency and asks for the fastest fuel it can get, which is usually sugar. This is not a willpower problem. It is a chemical one, and it repeats itself every single day until the underlying swing is addressed.

Weight that collects around your middle

High insulin specifically favours fat storage around the abdomen, even in women who are not significantly overweight. If your weight gain feels concentrated there rather than spread evenly, that is a common insulin pattern.

Skin changes

Some women notice darker, slightly velvety patches of skin around the neck, underarms or elbows. This is called acanthosis nigricans, and it is one of the more direct visible signs that insulin levels are running high. It is worth mentioning to your doctor if you notice it.

Feeling hungry soon after eating

If a full meal only keeps you satisfied for an hour, insulin swings are often the reason. Your blood sugar rises fast, then falls fast, and hunger returns before your next meal is anywhere close.

Not every woman with PCOS experiences all of these, and how strongly each one shows up depends on your hormonal type. Some women lean more towards insulin driven symptoms, others towards inflammation or thyroid driven ones. That is exactly why a generic plan so often misses the mark.

What actually helps

The goal is not to eliminate carbohydrates or eat as little as possible. That usually backfires, because it stresses the body further and can make cravings worse. What tends to help is steadying the swings.

  • Anchor meals with protein first. It slows down how fast sugar hits your bloodstream and softens the crash that follows.
  • Do not skip meals to save calories. Long gaps often make the next blood sugar spike worse, not better.
  • Pay attention to sleep and stress. Poor sleep and high cortisol both worsen insulin resistance, independent of what you eat.
  • Move after meals when you can. Even a short walk helps your muscles use up some of that blood sugar instead of it being stored.

None of this is about being stricter with yourself. It is about working with what your hormones are actually doing, instead of fighting them with more restriction.
